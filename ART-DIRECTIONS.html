Three Worlds, One Cone

Three visual directions, far enough apart that choosing is a real decision. Each comes with a reference set, a named palette, type with tabular numerals, a wordmark, and the same mocked hole — hole 7, par 4, water right — so the only thing that varies is the world, never the information.

The test every direction must pass. The cone and the hole plan share a scale and the ball can never finish outside the drawn cone. If a direction makes that shape decorative, the direction fails, however handsome.
All three mocks below use identical geometry — same fairway, same pond, same 235–285 driver cone clipping the water's edge.

Direction 01 · Tour broadcast, 1978

Sunday Tape

The final round as it reached a living room: a tower camera holding on a par 4, phosphor numbers burned into the corner of a tube that has been on since noon, a hand-set leaderboard worshipped like scripture. The world is dark, warm, and slightly degraded — and the cone lives here honestly, because drawing a shape over the picture to explain a shot is exactly what television invented the telestrator to do. The interface isn't pretending to be a broadcast; it's the coverage of your season.

Direction 02 · The architect's drawing room

Ink on Vellum

A golden-age course plan: iron-gall ink on cream vellum, watercolour laid in behind the linework, slopes hachured by hand, and every annotation that matters in the architect's red pen. The game becomes a folio of drawings you play — and the cone slots in without translation, because a red wedge marking intent on a plan is what red pens on plans have always done. Warmer and more handsome than the current caddie's yardage book, but the same bloodline: paper that means business.

Direction 03 · Municipal golf

The Muni

Cracked cart paths, chain-link behind the range, a hand-painted sign with one coat of paint, and a scorecard that has been photocopied since 1988. The whole interface is printed matter from the county rec department — two inks on cheap stock — with your shot drawn on in marker, because that is how anyone at a muni has ever explained a shot. Of the three, this is the one that sounds like the game's jokes: THE MUNI CHAMPIONSHIP is already on the schedule. Unpretentious, slightly sad, deeply loved.

Why it might win: it is the game's sense of humour made visible — recognition, not absurdity, in every texture — and flat two-ink printing is the cheapest style to produce and the hardest to make illegible. Marker orange on county green is the loudest cone of the three. The risk: "slightly sad" can slide into ugly-on-purpose. The rule that keeps it honest: the sadness lives in the words (— MGMT), never in the craft. Every screen should look like it came off a Risograph on purpose.

Choosing

What the game becomes in each world

Sunday TapeYour season as televised event. The leaderboard — already the game's proudest system — becomes the star of the art too. Boldest of the three, and the only dark one.
Ink on VellumYour season as a folio of drawings. The warmest evolution of what exists today, with the most legible cone. Lowest risk, least surprise.
The MuniYour season as county-printed matter. The only direction that looks the way the writing sounds. Highest tonal payoff, needs the most discipline to stay handsome.

A design lead's read, not a verdict: if the jokes are the soul of the game, The Muni is the bullseye. If the leaderboard is, Sunday Tape. If the yardage book already feels right, Ink on Vellum sharpens it rather than starting over. Whichever wins, three rules travel with it: the cone is always the only shape drawn in its colour; water, sand, fairway and rough keep one meaning everywhere they appear; and every numeral on screen is tabular, so yardage, score and money always land in columns.
