Title Defense

The bar, from the brief: a person who has never watched a golf tournament should understand it cold. Plus the tone rule every candidate inherits: if a golfer would say "oh no", it's right; if they'd say "what?", it's wrong. Four tests below: COLD (understood with zero golf), REGISTER (recognition, not absurdity), LOGIC (what rule of the game it states for free), CARD (how it sits in the Sunday Tape wordmark).

Keep Your Head Down

The only challenger that clears the full bar and adds something. It's golf's most universal piece of advice and, at the same time, the idiom for quietly surviving at work — which is the whole season layer in four words. A non-golfer hears the job; a golfer hears the swing thought; both are correct. What it trades away: the water, and with it the free tutorial the current title performs. Tour Card

The cleverest pun on the table — the document that is your job, in a game made of cards — and it fails the brief's one absolute test. A person who has never watched golf does not know a tour card is employment; the double meaning only lands after the game has taught it. The brief's bar exists precisely to kill this kind of insider elegance. Killed, with respect.

Fore!

The loudest card in the round, and thematically sharper than it first looks — it's the sound a miss makes, and this is a game about the shape of the miss. Register is a pure pass: "Fore!" is literally the shout of "oh no". But it stumbles twice. The cold test is shakier than its fame suggests: civilians know it as the golf yell without knowing it means "duck" — they understand the genre, not the meaning, and said aloud it's "Four?". And the field is crowded: there is already a game called Fore!, plus Hot Shots Golf Fore!, plus bundles and merchandise without end — it is the airport-gift-shop golf word, generic where this game's humour is specific. The paper-company-outing joke works because it's precise; "Fore!" is the mug that joke would come printed on.
ColdMIXED — famous as a sound, opaque as a meaning; homophone of "four" aloud
RegisterPASS — the canonical "oh no"

Steal the energy anyway: a phosphor FORE stamp when a shot finishes in the widest band of the cone is a free broadcast joke, and it teaches what the word means — art first, term last, exactly per the brief.

Killed at the door

The short list that didn't survive the bar

  • The Money List — states the death rule, but dry as a tax form; nobody says "oh no" at it.
  • Missed the Cut — semi-jargon, and announces failure without the joke that earns it.
  • Take Your Medicine — right register inside golf; cold reading is pharmaceutical.
  • Par Isn't Good Enough — it's pillar P7 wearing a title's clothes; reads as a lecture.
  • The Yips / Lay Up / Never Up, Never In — jargon. The brief's "what?" test, failed instantly.
  • Water on the Right — the incumbent, but weaker and with less music.
  • One Good Season — could be any sports story ever told; owns nothing.
Verdict

The title holds

Keep Don't Hit It In The Water. Nothing on the table beats a title that passes every test and teaches a third of the game before the first card is drawn. The only real challenger, Keep Your Head Down, adds the job but drops the water — and the water is the third the art is built around. A title change would also re-open the wordmark for no gained ground.

The round still pays: Keep Your Head Down is the named successor if the title ever has to change (a publisher, a trademark, a sequel). "That's in the water." should be stolen immediately as the water-ball result line in-game — it was always a flavour line auditioning as a title. And Fourteen Sundays goes in the drawer for the season-recap screen or the soundtrack.

Landscape check: the genre's named neighbours — Golfie, Cursed to Golf — both lean whimsical/supernatural. A recognition-register title is differentiated on sight, and no existing game holds any of the finalist names.
