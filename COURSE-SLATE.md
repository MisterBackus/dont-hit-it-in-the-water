# COURSE SLATE — identities for the next batch

**From:** the course-design side · 25 Aug 2026
**Status:** paper only. Nothing here is staged, built, or measured. Every number
below is an intention written before the harness gets a vote, and the house law
says a third of them are wrong. That is why they are written down.

**What this is.** The owner wants a large cycled pool — "20-30-40-50 courses
someday" — and SCHEDULE-PLAN.md turned that into a mechanism: courses register
with a tier and a fieldShift, events draw by constraint. Four courses exist.
This document proposes the identities for the next six, designed under
everything the dialogue has measured, so that when each one gets built it is
built the right way round: **distances first, hazards second, sequencing
third** (REVIEW-3 §3, REVIEW-4 §4, REVIEW-5 §1).

**The laws these are designed under**, so nobody has to re-derive them:

1. **A hole contains a decision only when reaching is in doubt.** The distance
   ladder creates decisions; hazards only price them (REVIEW-3, proven by the
   Ballwasher length experiment, 0.28 → 0.86 with nothing else touched).
2. **The stretch card's cone must carry a real price**, and the aim band is
   ±14 yards — only a hazard 12–25 off the line, long and thin and parallel,
   makes aim a choice (REVIEW-2 §3, confirmed across v3).
3. **The player must be able to afford the choice.** The focus shadow is one
   hole long; fork chains starve themselves; funding rounds are design
   material, not breathers; difficulty added at the front leaks out the back
   (CHANGES-5, REVIEW-5, REVIEW-6 §3 — the Kiln, 0.36 → 0.78 on sequencing
   alone).
4. **The doubt must live in the picture, not in the dice** (REVIEW-6 §5).
   Until DEPTH-DECISION's Option A lands, carry-jitter gambles are invisible
   to players and policies alike, and any design leaning on ±5% of carry is a
   hidden coin flip. Two entries below are gated on that work and say so.
5. **The bar:** REAL = gap ≥ 0.35 AND split ≥ 60%. A front four wants two
   holes clearing it and at most one flat, no par 5, par 31–33 total, and the
   softlock scan at zero — the record is six reviews old and it stays that way.
6. The measured ladder these slot into: **Rockdale −1.65 · Pine Hollow +0.71 ·
   Cottonwood +1.77 · Salt Flats +3.02** (mixed, full round, vs par).

The four existing questions, which nothing below may repeat: Pine Hollow *"do
you own the number?"* · Cottonwood *"do you trust the line?"* · Rockdale *"can
you convert?"* · Salt Flats *"will you stretch?"*

Ladder used for every subtraction (finishes): **277 · 255 · 208 · 170 · 133 ·
105 · 94 · 70 · 58 · 55 · 35.** Technique fits that recur below, priced from
cards.ts: MI+15 = 185 (Extra Club, free, ×1.35) · LI−15 = 193 (Smooth, 1
focus, half cone) · LI−25 = 183 (Choke, 1 focus, ×0.65) · MI−15 = 155
(Smooth, 1 focus, half cone) · FW+40 = 145 (Rip, 1 focus, doubled) · LI+15 =
223 (Extra Club, free, ×1.35). The cluster at 145/148/155 and 183/185/193 is the standing "two
entries in the lookup" hazard from REVIEW-2 — every entry below that leans on
those leaves says how it prices them.

# 1. PALMETTO — the tempter

**Home event:** The Sunbelt Open (event 1, which currently borrows Rockdale).
**Tier:** gentle. Opener-safe at cone ×1.40, check-week-safe. Par 33.

**Identity: HOW MUCH DO YOU WANT IT?** Every tee offers a buyout — the big
card purchases a whole shot class, not just yards — and every buyout has
exactly one string attached, always visible, never fatal. Rockdale asks
whether you can convert the chances a short course gives everyone; Palmetto
asks how many chances you insist on creating. The adjacency is real and
acknowledged — see the honest section — but the tests differ: Rockdale sorts
on execution, Palmetto sorts on appetite, and at a place-based cut those are
different distributions.

**The world.** A sunbelt resort that waters the fairways twice a day.
Fairway: overfed emerald, runs true. Rough: bermuda tangle that grabs ankles
and run-out alike. Bunkers: white sugar sand, raked for the brochure.
Water: lagoons — every one of them photogenic, every one placed by the
Retention Pond principle (off the direct line, pricing only the cone edge
that chose to live near it). Trees: palm clumps, more shade than menace, one
of them load-bearing. OB: the resort itself — pool deck and cart barn down
one side of one hole, because somebody always finds the pool deck.

**Engine carriers.** Position upside (REVIEW-3's second shape — the proven
generator at short lengths: Cart Path Only, The Oak); aim-band strips at
12–25 off the line; the surface-dependent run-out rule on one par 5, where a
chasing second's middle pitches on the green and sits while its edges pitch
beside it and run 30 into trouble — the cone's middle and edges finishing
twenty yards apart, all of it visible in the picture. Nothing gated.

# 2. MEADOWLARK — the second-shot course

**Home event:** The Fall Series (event 9 — the check-2 week, which
SCHEDULE-PLAN wants to be about conversion, "did you bank when banking was
offered").
**Tier:** gentle-mid. Check-week-safe. Par 31.

**Identity: IS CLOSER ACTUALLY BETTER?** The tee shots are free — wide
corridors, almost nothing to hit — and every decision lives in what the tee
shot *leaves*. The course is built on the one rule in the engine nobody has
designed with: **shortening widens the cone** — `spread × (1 + takeOff ×
2.2)` (REVIEW-1 §4.4). A Full Wedge cut to 85 is half again as wide as a
full one; a Splash cut to 45 is wider than a Mid Iron. So Meadowlark's
leaves are engineered in pairs: the longer club leaves the widened partial,
the modest club leaves the exact number, and "hit it as far as you can" —
the reflex every other course rewards or punishes with hazards — is here
simply *wrong on the arithmetic*, sometimes. Not always. That's the game:
you do the subtraction on every tee, and the course alternates which answer
is right.

Pine Hollow adjacency, acknowledged: both are ladder-arithmetic courses.
Pine Hollow asks which card reaches the number; Meadowlark asks what the
number you reach *leaves*, one shot deeper. Different rung, same machine —
flagged honestly in the capacity section.

**The world.** A course mown out of hay meadows an hour from anywhere.
Fairway: cut meadow, generous. Rough: the hay itself — they baled in June,
it is August. Water: a creek that wanders through twice, in thin strips.
Bunkers: three, old, blown in at the edges. Deep: the unmown far banks.
No OB anywhere; the worst thing that happens at Meadowlark is a lie.

**Engine carriers.** The cut-down widening formula, front and centre; the
free-shot floor (CHIP_RANGE 60 — sub-60 leaves are never dead, so the bad
leaves can be genuinely bad without softlock risk); aim-band creek strips on
two holes; the 205 par-3 slot, rationed to one, at the offset that measured
(Ballwasher forked at −10, Postcard didn't at +18 — the open diagnostic —
so Meadowlark's copy sits at −8 and doubles as a data point).

target, and it is the honest price of the gentle tier (Rockdale finished at
two REAL total in front). If the harness agrees, the first fix is hole 1's
flank bunkers moving 2 yards toward the line, inside the aim band.

**Prediction.** Mean −0.4. The widening deltas are modest (±6 vs ±9-ish) and
the depth die is invisible pre-Option-A, so Meadowlark's spread will come
from conversion rates, not disasters — which is what a check week wants.
Registered risk: if the harness says the partial-widening differences are
too small to move scores, the identity needs leaves pushed into the 71–85
band (takeoff ≥ 0.2) on two more holes, not new hazards.

# 3. DRIFTWOOD — the links

**Home event:** Coastal Classic (event 13 — SCHEDULE-PLAN's own note says
"Coastal" deserves better than a borrowed parkland; this is the course that
earns the name without touching Salt Flats' majors-only doctrine).
**Tier:** mid. General rotation; acceptable late-season. Par 32.

**Identity: DO YOU FLY IT OR RUN IT?** The wind-implied course, without wind
mechanics. Everything about it says exposed coast — and every hole offers an
air route and a ground route that are both defensible and differently
shaped, which is the definition of a fork. The engine already contains an
entire ground game nobody has built a course on: run-out is full on short
grass, ×0.40 in rough, ×0.30 when the pitch lands on the green (REVIEW-1
§4.1); the Stinger finishes 255 only when it pitches on fairway; rest-judged
surfaces gate running shots while wedges fly free. Salt Flats uses the crust
to *refuse* the runner. Driftwood is the inversion: here the runner is a
first-class citizen and the question is whether today's hole, and today's
lie, is a running day.

**The world.** Hardpan links turf that runs like a cart path. Fairway: the
running ground. Rough: marram grass — *the grass that ends the running
game*, because a pitch in it halves the run and the ball dies where it
lands. Deep: gorse, which keeps whatever reaches it. Bunkers: pots, small
enough to be personal, sod-faced, placed 12–25 off the line where aim is a
real answer. OB: the beach, one side of two holes. Water: a single tidal
slough at the last. Greens: open in front — the front door is always
unlocked; the trouble flanks.

**Engine carriers.** The run-shear fork, which deserves naming because it is
new: **a running shot's rest point depends on its pitch surface, so lateral
scatter converts into depth scatter of the finish** — the cone's middle
pitches turf and runs 37; its edge pitches marram and dies 22 shorter, with
the trouble sitting between the two rest points. The doubt is lateral, in
the picture, fully legal under law 4 — and it gets even more legible when
Option A draws the run-out tail, which this course benefits from but does
not require. Also: pitch-point water at the closer; pots in the aim band;
open-fronted greens making the ground route real on approaches.

**GATED footnote:** true crosswind — a per-hole lateral push that moves the
whole cone — would be this course's natural second axis and **needs an
engine axis that does not exist.** Nothing below assumes it.

# 4. FOXGLOVE — the hunt club

**Home event:** The Founders Cup (proposed name, for whenever the calendar
grows a slot; until then Foxglove registers as a host venue for unnamed
mid-season invitationals). Never a check week.
**Tier:** mid. Par 32.

**Identity: WHAT WILL YOU PAY FOR THE RIGHT NUMBER?** Every fork on the
course asks a number that sits in the ladder's seams — 145, 155, 185, 193,
214, 223 — where naked cards putt from the fringe and the *fits* all cost
something: nothing but width (Extra Club, free, ×1.35), a focus for
tightness (Smooth, Choke), or a stroke for patience (the lay-up). Pine
Hollow asks whether you own the number; Foxglove asks what you'll pay to
rent it. The focus economy — regen, momentum, the shadow — is not the
constraint the course fights; it *is* the course.

**Why this course is also an instrument.** REVIEW-6 §8.3 raised the
escalation nobody has tested: the 1-focus tight fits may be priced so low
that "stretch" is never actually refused. Foxglove is that question built
as eight holes. **Pre-registered:** if the harness shows every Foxglove fork
resolving into the same Extra-Club lookup — high split nowhere, everyone
buying the free loose fit — the finding is that technique pricing, not
course geometry, caps the game's decision count, and the dialogue formally
escalates from course design to card design. Either result is worth having.

**The world.** Old money, walled grounds, a club that has never once been in
a hurry. Fairway: clipped parkland avenues. Rough: fescue kept exactly long
enough to disapprove. Trees: specimen oaks, each one deliberate. Bunkers:
Victorian pits, deep, faces revetted — recoverable, expensive. Water: one
ornamental pond that has drowned more members than balls. OB: the boundary
wall, one hole. Notes in the voice of a steward who has seen your kind
before.

**Engine carriers.** The technique system as the identity (cards.ts:
Smooth/Choke/Extra/Rip and their exact fits); focus regen and the shadow as
load-bearing sequencing; aim-band flanks pricing every loose fit — the free
×1.35 stretch must always have both bands live, or it is a free lookup and
the hole dies (the Postcard lesson, twice measured).

# 5. BRACKEN RIDGE — the forest

**Home events:** THE PGA AT BRACKEN RIDGE (event 11) and the Bracken Ridge
Classic (event 8) — the named venue that has been wearing Cottonwood's
course all season. SCHEDULE-PLAN open question 3 called it "the strongest
candidate for a fifth course: a named venue with a major and no home." Cash
that cheque.
**Tier:** hard. **Major-capable.** Not check-week-safe, never the opener.
Par 32.

**Identity: HOW MUCH FAIRWAY DO YOU NEED?** The width course. Every other
course prices decisions with hazard ellipses; Bracken Ridge prices them
with the corridor itself — pinches at the stretch card's landing zone, width
at the lay-up's, and the free surface bands (rough at +12, deep at +26,
trees at +40; geometry.ts) doing the collecting. The question on every tee
is your cone width against the corridor width *at the distance you chose*.
Pine Hollow asks about the number; Bracken asks about the number's width.
A ±32 card into a 15-yard pinch is a different proposition from a ±13 card
into the same pinch, and the course is built so that the narrow place and
the awkward-leave place are never the same place.

**The Cottonwood lesson, applied.** Corridor pricing is symmetric — it
punishes both edges — and symmetric punishment produced Cottonwood's
variance-without-decisions problem. So Bracken's pinches sit only at
*chosen* landing zones (the stretch card's finish, never the whole hole),
every pinch has a wide alternative at a different distance, and the fern is
the price band, not the trees: `deep` at ×2.6 spread is nearly a lost hole,
`trees` at ×2.4 is one, and the brief's own warning (§3: "use it
deliberately, and rarely") is obeyed — placed tree ellipses appear twice on
the course, both on diagonals, both cleared by 30+ yards of rest margin
(law 4: no dice-doubt margins).

**The world.** Old growth and fern. Fairway: needle-carpet avenues, quick
and quiet. Rough: fern fringe. Deep: the bracken itself — the course is
named after its hazard, and the notes should let the player work that out.
Trees: the stands, which are walls. Bunkers: rare, old, sod-faced. Water:
one creek, crossing once. OB: nothing — the forest does not need it.

**Engine carriers.** Corridor interpolation as the pricing mechanism (the
free-band ladder off `corridorHalf`); cone-width arithmetic per card;
reach-doubt lengths from the proven zones (the Grind's 465-class tee shot,
the 205–218 par-3 slot, the wedge-gated five); one Oak-transplant diagonal.

# 6. RIVERMOUTH — the carry course · **DEPTH-GATED**

**Home events:** Rivermouth Open (event 5) and Highwater Open (event 10) —
the two water-named events currently faked by Cottonwood and Pine Hollow.
**Tier:** mid-hard. General rotation once built. Par 32.
**GATE: do not build before DEPTH-DECISION Option A lands — all four
implementation steps, but especially the policy step.** Every fork below is
a carry priced against a pitch band. Under today's engine that is dice-doubt
by definition (REVIEW-6 §5: the ±5% depth die is invisible to the picture
and the policies; The Crossing failed three times proving it). Under Option
A the pitch band is drawn, the policies read it, and "how much carry do you
buy" becomes exactly the design space the spec predicts will open. This
course is the reason to want that work, written down as eight holes.

**Identity: HOW MUCH CARRY DO YOU BUY?** Every hole crosses water, and every
crossing is priced in yards of pitch carry — with the buy always available
three ways: naked (your card's band clips the far edge), certain (+15 or
+40, wider cone for more carry), or refused (the dry lay, a stroke of
patience). Salt Flats asks the question with crust and refusal; Rivermouth
asks it with water and purchase. The Stinger is refused all day by the
pitch-point rule — the game's namesake card, dead on the course named for
water, which is the course's joke and its teeth.

**The world.** A river delta braided through levee turf. Water: the braids —
the course's whole voice. Deep: reed beds and mud flats, rest-judged, for
the ball that cleared the water and nothing else. Fairway: the levees.
Rough: wet fringe. OB: the far bank and the harbor, sparingly. Every
crossing flyable by a wedge from short of it — the softlock law holds by
construction, checked hole by hole before any file is written.

**Engine carriers (post-gate).** Pitch-judged water and OB for lowFlight
(types.ts `lowFlight`; the Stinger refusal); the drawn pitch band as the
contract the player buys against; depth-aware policies making the three

# The honest section — how many courses can this engine hold?

The owner asked whether 50 is realistic. Real answer, in three parts.

## 1. Questions vs venues

Count the decision generators the engine actually contains — the moves that
have measured REAL at least once, plus the two this slate stakes out:

| Generator | Proof | Claimed by |
|---|---|---|
| A. Reach-or-lay (ladder gap) | Ballwasher 0.88, Kiln 0.78, Long One | Pine Hollow, everyone's par-3 slot |
| B. Aim-band strip | Cart Path Only 0.82 | Cottonwood v5 holes; support everywhere |
| C. Position upside (short 4) | Oak 0.63, Cart Path | Rockdale, Palmetto |
| D. Run / rest-judged gating | SF crossings, Verdict 0.43 | Salt Flats (refusal), **Driftwood** (embrace) |
| E. Focus ledger / sequencing | The Kiln experiment | **Foxglove** (as identity), all (as law) |
| F. Corridor-width pricing | unproven — the Grind's 0.41 is adjacent | **Bracken Ridge** (the bet) |
| G. Partial-widening (leave quality) | unproven — engine rule, never designed on | **Meadowlark** (the bet) |
| H. Carry pricing (pitch band) | GATED on Option A | **Rivermouth** |
| I. Tail pricing (run-out band) | GATED on Option A | unclaimed |

Ten courses — the four built and these six — roughly cover A through H. What
remains unclaimed on the current engine: a pure aim-band course ("which side
do you miss on?" — eight Cart Paths risks monotony but is available), an
OB-economics course ("the +2 course" — greed priced double), and the
tail-pricing course (firm greens, trouble behind, wedges safe — needs Option
A's drawn tail to be picture-honest). Call it **12–13 genuinely distinct
questions on the current engine, and this slate deliberately spends most of
the remaining ones.** Two of the six (Bracken, Meadowlark) are bets on
unproven generators, and that is disclosed in their entries.

## 2. What each future axis unlocks

- **Depth bands (Option A — already decided, queued).** Unlocks Rivermouth
  and the tail-pricing course outright, upgrades Driftwood's run-shear and
  every advertised "sneak" line from lottery ticket to honest fork, and
  reopens The Crossing. Worth 3–4 identities and a legibility upgrade to
  every existing one. The cheapest identity-per-engineering-hour on the
  board, and it is already paid for.
- **Wind.** The biggest single unlock, because it is two axes in one: a
  headwind term re-asks *every existing course's question at new numbers*
  (the ladder shifts per hole — Pine Hollow's 442 becomes a different hole
  at −15), and a crosswind term moves the aim band (Driftwood's true second
  axis, and a "which side do you miss on" course that isn't monotonous).
  Worth 3–4 new identities plus effective re-releases of the whole pool.
  Real engine work: the cone must move honestly (P8) and determinism rules
  (SCHEDULE-PLAN §5: no per-course RNG) mean wind must be data, not rolls.
- **Elevation.** A per-hole carry scale — a subset of headwind's math, worth
  1–2 identities (the mountain course, the punchbowl) unless combined with
  depth bands, where uphill-kills-run and downhill-stretches-tail gets
  interesting.
- **Green shapes / pin positions.** Quietly the most valuable for the
  *existing* problem set: proximity is why every par 3 under 200 measures
  flat (REVIEW-3 §3 — "a par 3 that everyone reaches can only offer
  proximity"). A back-right pin behind a tongue turns a 165 from a lookup
  into a fork, and would break the 205–218 monopoly on par-3 decisions —
  the single scarcest slot in the game (three courses already converge on
  205; this slate rations it strictly, one per course, at 205/212/214/218
  spacing). Worth 2–3 identities and a rescue for the short par 3 as a
  category.
- **Conditions (firm/soft, seasonal).** A run-out multiplier per event.
  Fewer new identities (1–2) than re-asks: firm Pine Hollow and soft
  Driftwood are different weeks at zero course-design cost. Pairs naturally
  with the pool mechanism — same venue, different answer.

## 3. So: is 50 realistic?

**Fifty distinct questions? No — and it does not matter.** The realistic
ceiling is 12–13 questions on the current engine and roughly 20–22 with all
the axes above landed. **Fifty *courses* is entirely realistic**, because a
course is a question × a world × a tier × a sequencing, and the real tour
proves the model: the Open rota is nine links asking maybe three questions
between them, and nobody minds, because venue, length, and weather make each
week legible as itself. The pool mechanism is built for exactly this — the
player meets fourteen courses a season, drawn by tier, so two courses
sharing a generator at different tiers and worlds (Rockdale and Palmetto;
Salt Flats and Rivermouth) never read as repeats inside one run.

The honest constraints on the road to 50, in order of bite:

1. **Gentle-tier scarcity.** Only three generators build gentle courses
   (conversion, temptation, leave-arithmetic). The pool needs roughly one
   gentle course per four entries for the opener and check-week draws. This
   is the tier to commission deliberately, not the one that happens by
   accident — hard courses are the easy ones to invent.
2. **The par-3 slot.** One fork-capable window (205–218) until green shapes
   or a mid-iron-range reward card widens it. Every course gets one; a
   50-course pool will have fifty par 3s asking the same 13 yards of
   question unless that axis lands.
3. **The technique cluster.** 145/148/155 and 183/185/193 are each three
   solutions to one number. Foxglove will measure whether that pricing caps
   decision density game-wide; if it does, the fix is one card-cost pass,
   and it raises the ceiling for every course at once.
4. **Field-response first.** Unchanged from REVIEW-1 §5 through
   SCHEDULE-PLAN §3: no new course enters the schedule before the field
   feels course difficulty. Every entry above ships with a predicted
   fieldShift for exactly that reason.

Recommended build order, given all of it: **Palmetto** (fills the scarcest
tier, exercises the pool's gentle draw), **Driftwood** (new generator, no
gate, mid tier is where the pool is thinnest per slot), **Bracken Ridge**
(the named venue debt and the second major — after field-response lands),
**Meadowlark**, **Foxglove**, and **Rivermouth the week Option A ships.**

## Craft notes for whoever builds these (possibly me)

1. **Check every technique fit against cards.ts at build time, not from
   this document.** The fits quoted here (185/193/183/155/145/223) were
   computed from the current card file; one balance pass on techniques
   invalidates every one of them, and Foxglove's §8.3 experiment may cause
   exactly that pass.
2. **No rest-judged blocker within 15 yards of an intended rest point**
   until Option A lands (the ±5% die is ±13 on a Bomb and invisible). The
   Oak's 9-yard margin predates the law and survives on grandfather rights;
   nothing new gets one.
3. **Every "some"-tier hole is allowed to stay "some."** The bar is five
   REAL per course nowhere except Salt Flats' own header; the standing
   target is two in the front four. Not every hole must fork — Rockdale 8
   and Respite are load-bearing precedents, and the funding rounds above
   are flat *on purpose*.
4. **Write the reach-cost table before placing a hazard** (REVIEW-3 §5), and
   pre-register every prediction the way CHANGES-3 did — the zero-for-four
   round and the Kiln both came from predictions specific enough to be
   wrong in useful directions.
5. **The notes carry the humour; the humour is recognition.** If a golfer
   would say "oh no," it is right. Palmetto's fountain, Meadowlark's hay,
   Foxglove's steward, the beach at Driftwood — none of these is a joke.
   All of them are a feeling.
